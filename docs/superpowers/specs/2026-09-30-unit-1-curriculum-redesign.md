# Unit 1 Curriculum Redesign

**Date:** 2026-09-30  
**Status:** Approved design  
**Scope:** Sixth grade Unit 1 curricular content

## Purpose

Rewrite Unit 1 so every subject has a coherent learning progression and every learning week has a focused investigation. The generator topic describes authentic intersections between subjects; it does not force all subjects into one narrative.

The redesign keeps the application engine, lesson DSL, schedule, progress model, activity registry, CNB catalog, validators, and media replacement system. It rewrites the Unit 1 curricular layer: subject lessons, weekly metadata, workshops, challenges, item banks, examples, interactions, and media briefs.

## Fixed Constraints

- Preserve complete Unit 1 CNB coverage. Required content may move between weeks inside Unit 1, but it may not be removed or moved to another unit.
- Preserve the weekly schedule: 27 subject lessons, one interdisciplinary workshop, and one weekly challenge.
- Preserve the expected lesson counts for all ten subjects.
- Preserve lesson and media identifier conventions unless a changed identifier is necessary to describe genuinely new content.
- Keep lessons usable without a teacher and appropriate for students aged 11-12.
- Keep Guatemala-specific examples accurate, respectful, multicultural, and relevant.
- Keep sensitive health and recent-history content scientific, age-appropriate, and suitable for human review.

## Chosen Approach

Use a progression-first rewrite. Each subject follows its own sound sequence. Weekly investigations are built afterward from two to four subjects that overlap naturally.

Rejected alternatives:

- Rewriting only weekly wrappers would preserve disconnected subject content.
- Making every subject serve every weekly project would reproduce forced integration under a new name.

Existing explanations, activities, and questions may be retained only after review confirms that they fit the new progression and quality rules. No existing lesson is accepted automatically.

## Unit Arc

**Unit title:** Conocemos nuestro lugar para cuidarlo y convivir

The unit moves from locating and describing the student's surroundings toward investigating, caring for, and communicating about the community. Each learning week has an independent central question and a practical product.

| Week | Central investigation | Weekly product |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | How do we locate and present the place where we live? | Annotated local map and short oral presentation |
| 2 | How do we organize a healthy and respectful market? | Plan for a safe school market stand |
| 3 | How do we make the journey to school safer? | Route map, safety analysis, and public sign |
| 4 | How do we grow, care for ourselves, and participate? | Student-created informational scene or campaign |
| 5 | How do we design a school everyone can navigate? | Accessible tactile school map |
| 6 | How do we choose a nutritious snack using local resources? | Nutrition proposal with comparison and budget |
| 7 | How do we investigate and protect community water? | Evidence-based community water report |
| 8 | How do we communicate reliable data to save energy? | School energy information display |

The Spanish titles and student-facing wording will be finalized while rewriting each week. The central question and product above are binding; wording may change without changing their educational intent.

## Subject Progressions

### Mathematics

Plane geometry and congruence; polygons and patterns; transformations and perimeter; solids; integers and coordinates; sets; large numbers and number systems; approximation and Mayan numeration.

Mathematics uses contexts that represent the concept rather than decorate it. Geometry uses manipulable figures and construction; integers use number lines and temperature; coordinates use maps and plotted points; sets use diagrams; numerical systems use place-value and Mayan numeral builders.

### Communication and Language L1

Oral presentation; efficient reading; locating information; dramatic texts; precise description and grammar; research planning; research questions and source selection; notes, citations, revision, and final reports.

Vocabulary, reading, grammar, writing, and speaking within a lesson share one meaningful context. Writing includes a model, criteria, drafting, and revision where appropriate.

### Natural Science and Technology

Origins and cells; heredity and living organisms; biological diversity and glands; reproductive health and ethical care; drug prevention and nutrition; environmental health; water, forests, and population; energy, climate, and scientific investigation.

Lessons follow phenomenon or question, scientific model, guided interpretation, and application. Experiments must be safe for home use. Complex mechanisms use clear diagrams or production-ready animation mocks.

### Social Studies

Geographic location and climate; population and cultural heritage; routes and exchange; work and social organization; ancient civilizations; Guatemala's historical conflicts; peace and regional cooperation; public institutions, taxation, and historical memory.

Lessons use maps, timelines, sources, causes, consequences, and multiple perspectives. Dates, places, peoples, and institutions must be verifiable.

### L2

Listening and prediction; respectful interaction; interpreting signs; dramatic and oral expression; opinions supported by reasons; pronunciation contrasts; rhythm and poetry; communicating researched information.

Activities prioritize meaningful listening, speaking, reading, and writing. Material involving a Mayan language remains a production mock until validated and recorded by a native speaker.

### English L3

Practical classroom language; market interactions; signs and directions; sound-writing relationships; description; short connected texts; past events; accessible biography.

Instructions remain in Spanish and target language stays short, correct, and useful. Pronunciation media briefs include the exact script to record.

### Citizenship Education

Solidarity and rights; equity and diversity; confronting discrimination; democratic leadership; school participation; identifying violence; dialogue and peace; memory, justice, and inclusion.

Lessons use realistic school and community decisions. Attitudinal content requires dilemmas, comparison, reflection, or a proposed action rather than a single informational card.

### Artistic Expression

Musical notation; rhythm and melody; graphic communication; performance; environmental textures; tactile creation; mural composition; publication and cultural presentation.

Each week includes practical creation with accessible household materials. Visual work uses drawing or composition interactions where available and explicit project instructions otherwise.

### Physical Education

Body control and balance; speed and coordination; rhythm and spatial movement; throwing; passing and control; safety and relaxation; teamwork and rules; equitable leadership and traditional games.

Lessons are active and include warm-up, modeled technique, timed practice, inclusive adaptation, hydration or safety guidance, and cool-down where appropriate.

### Productivity and Development

Community development; entrepreneurship; problem analysis; environment and production; safe tool use; project planning; environmental solutions; conservation action.

Students analyze concrete cases and produce small plans, budgets, comparisons, or project artifacts instead of answering only factual questions.

## Lesson Design

Each subject lesson teaches one central idea in 10-15 minutes and normally contains 9-14 steps:

1. Brief observation, prediction, movement, or problem.
2. Explicit teaching with examples and counterexamples.
3. Guided practice with hints and explanatory feedback.
4. Independent application in a believable context.
5. Two new exit-ticket items without hints.
6. Reflection only when it adds educational value.

No scored activity may depend on content that has not already been taught. The final lesson of a subject's week may include mixed and spiral practice.

## Interaction Design

Choose interaction types from the student's learning action:

- Mathematics: polygons, coordinate maps, number lines, set diagrams, charts, sequences, recipes when proportional reasoning is relevant, and Mayan numeral builders.
- Language: annotated reading, ordering, matching evidence, oral-response planning, guided writing, editing, and source comparison.
- Science: labeled models, classification, safe experiments, causal animations, timelines, and scenario decisions.
- Social studies: maps, timelines, source comparison, cause-and-effect structures, and civic dilemmas.
- Art and physical education: drawing, rhythm, movement sequences, technique demonstrations, and practical creation.
- Workshops: maps, investigations, designs, budgets, reports, campaigns, or physical products.

Multiple choice checks understanding but does not replace construction, manipulation, writing, mapping, or decision-making. Do not repeat the same interaction type more than twice in succession unless the repetition is integral to deliberate practice.

## Weekly Workshop and Challenge

Each workshop combines only two to four subjects with a real contribution to the weekly product. It reviews and applies content already taught that week and introduces no new CNB content.

Each weekly challenge contains new, unhinted items from at least six subjects. Items assess what the subject lessons taught, not merely vocabulary from the generator topic. The item bank contains new material for later Unit 1 validation.

## Visual and Media Policy

- Use a descriptive Lucide icon for a recognizable concrete object when one exists.
- Do not represent tortillas, windows, buildings, wells, food, tools, or places with generic circles or squares.
- Use geometric shapes only when geometry itself is the learning content.
- Use detailed SVG or diagrammatic activities for mathematical and scientific structure when the existing DSL supports them.
- Use a media mock when the ideal resource requires substantial illustration, animation, audio, video, cultural validation, or new simulation work.
- Every mock includes a self-contained production brief, meaningful alternative text, dimensions or duration, and the exact educational behavior expected.
- The media backlog must continue to expose the target file path and `MEDIA_ASSETS` replacement snippet through the internal registry and Medios screen.

A mock is acceptable when it clearly specifies the intended final resource. A weak permanent substitute is not acceptable merely because the final resource is expensive.

## Implementation Boundaries

The rewrite changes Unit 1 content files and may add narrowly scoped tests or audit scripts required to enforce these rules. It does not redesign navigation, progress storage, the lesson engine, the general UI, or Units 2-4.

Implementation proceeds week by week. For each week:

1. Build a CNB coverage matrix and identify content moved from another Unit 1 week.
2. Rewrite the relevant subject lessons while preserving exact lesson counts.
3. Rewrite weekly metadata, workshop, challenge, and bank after subject lessons are stable.
4. Validate and review the week's agenda before starting the next week.

Week 9 project and Week 10 generated validation are reviewed after Weeks 1-8 because they depend on the completed unit.

## Verification and Acceptance

Each completed week must pass:

- Structural and CNB validation with no new errors.
- Type checking and unit tests.
- Review for ambiguous answers, duplicate correct options, and unsupported assessment content.
- Interaction-variety audit and generic-object-icon audit.
- Media brief and replacement-registry audit.
- Agenda review confirming that the workshop uses previously taught material.

After each practical group of weeks, run mobile and tablet lesson walkthroughs. Before completing Unit 1, run the full validator, TypeScript check, unit tests, production build, and Unit 1 end-to-end walkthrough.

The redesign is complete when all Unit 1 CNB content remains covered, every required lesson exists, the eight workshops match the approved investigations, no new validation errors remain, and the full Unit 1 experience passes its automated checks.
